# Module 2: Diagnose before you fix

**Outcome:** you can name the one thing holding your profit back and pick the right kind of fix.

**Related SOPs:** [03 Constraint diagnosis](../sops/03-constraint-diagnosis.md)


<!-- plain:start -->
> **In plain words**
>
> **Stage:** Grow (keep more of every sale, then scale). Every lesson below is tagged with its stage; see [Attract, Convert, Grow](stages.md).
>
> **What it is:** Before you change anything, find the one thing holding profit back.
>
> **Why it matters:** Fixing the wrong thing wastes weeks. Usually only one problem is the real blocker at a time.
>
> **Do this:**
>
> 1. Look at your numbers: visits, conversion rate, order value and ad costs.
> 2. Find the weakest one compared with a healthy range.
> 3. Fix only that one this week, then check again.
>
> **Words to know:**
>
> - **Conversion rate:** Out of every 100 visits, how many end in an order. 2% means 2 orders per 100 visits.
> - **AOV:** Average order value: total sales divided by number of orders.
> - **CPA:** Cost per acquisition: how much ad money it took to get one sale.
>
> All words are explained in the [glossary](../glossary.md).

## Lesson 2.1: Three kinds of constraint
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** A constraint is the one thing limiting your profit right now. Fixing anything else first wastes time and money. Many stores are short of sales even when the owner thinks they have an "ads problem".

**Step 1.** Open the Dashboard on **Month**. Write down orders, net sales, MER and contribution margin (contribution profit divided by net sales).

**Expected result:** You have four numbers to diagnose from.

<!-- do:growth -->

**Step 2.** Match them to one of the three kinds in the table below. **Throughput** means not enough sales. **Efficiency** means costs are too high. **Capacity** means you cannot keep up with orders.

**Expected result:** You have named one constraint.

**Step 3.** Write the constraint at the top of your decision log and keep it there until it is fixed.

**Expected result:** Every change you make this month can be checked against that one constraint.

### Good to know

| Kind | What it looks like | What fixes it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Throughput** (not enough sales) | Your cost ratios are healthy, but you get too few orders. | More visitors, better ads, a better-converting site, new channels. |
| **Efficiency** (costs too high) | One or more cost ratios are too high. | Better prices, cheaper product cost, cheaper shipping, bigger orders, better ads. |
| **Capacity** (cannot keep up) | Products sell out, cash runs short, orders ship slowly. | Stock planning, a cash forecast, better supplier terms, faster fulfilment. |

## Lesson 2.2: The five situations
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** The same tactic can help one store and hurt another. Your scorecard puts you in one of five situations, and each has a different next move. Picking the right one stops you scaling a leaky store or cutting a healthy one.

**Step 1.** Check your net profit % for the last 30 days on the Dashboard. If it is 10% or more, your profit is **healthy**. Grow, and win every customer you can serve at a profit.

**Expected result:** You know whether you are in the healthy group.

<!-- do:growth -->

**Step 2.** If profit is under 10%, find the cost share that is too high. **Fixed costs heavy:** grow carefully, because doubling sales halves their share. **Variable costs heavy:** pause growth and fix cost per order first. **Marketing heavy:** MER is high, so find where buyers drop out before you touch the ads (see Lesson 2.5).

**Expected result:** You have picked one of the three cost situations.

**Step 3.** If every number is borderline, follow this order: fix MER first, then VCR (order costs), then grow.

**Expected result:** You have a written order of work for the next month.

## Lesson 2.3: Fixing variable costs
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Variable costs come with every order, so a small fix pays back on every sale for good. Most stores can take several points off without customers noticing. Work down the list and stop when VCR is back under about 45%.

**Step 1.** Email your main supplier. Ask for the price at the next order size up, and a price for simpler packaging.

**Expected result:** You have a written price. Compare it with the cash a bigger order would tie up (MOQ means minimum order quantity).

**Step 2.** If shipping is over 15% of sales, ask two carriers for prices. Give them last month's parcel count and weights.

**Expected result:** You have two quotes to compare with your current cost per order.

**Watch out:** Boxes that fit the product better often save more than a new carrier. Many carriers charge by size as well as weight.

**Step 3.** Set free shipping about 20 to 30% above your average order value, a common rule of thumb. Charge for express delivery (Shopify: **Settings > Shipping and delivery**).

**Expected result:** New orders either pay for shipping or grow to qualify for free shipping.

**Step 4.** Put your higher-margin products in the first row of your home page and in your ads. Margin is the share of the price you keep after product cost.

**Expected result:** Sales mix shifts towards products that leave more profit per order.

**Step 5.** Test a small price rise of 3 to 5% on one best seller for two weeks.

**Expected result:** You can compare conversion rate and profit per order before and after.

**Step 6.** Raise your order size and trim fees.

1. Add a bundle or a "buy more, save more" deal (see Module 9).
2. In Shopify, look at payment and app fees for last month. Divide them by sales.

**Expected result:** Average order value goes up. Payment fees sit close to your card rate, and you have cut apps you do not use.

## Lesson 2.4: Fixing fixed costs
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Fixed costs stay the same however many orders you get. Cutting waste helps, but the bigger fix is often growing sales so fixed costs become a smaller share. Do both deliberately.

**Step 1.** Download your bank and card statements for the last 3 months. List every subscription and what it costs each month.

**Expected result:** You have one list of every recurring charge.

**Step 2.** Cancel every app or tool nobody used in the last 30 days.

**Expected result:** Your monthly fixed costs drop by the total of the cancelled tools.

**Step 3.** For each role and agency, write one line on what it makes. Note if that links to a result you can measure.

**Expected result:** You know which costs earn their keep and which need a conversation.

**Step 4.** Update **Fixed costs per month** in **Your numbers** with the new total.

**Expected result:** Net profit on the Dashboard reflects the lower costs from today.

## Lesson 2.5: Fixing marketing costs (high MER)
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** High MER means ads eat too much of your sales. Changing the ads is often the wrong fix. The problem can be the site, the offer or a short-term shock. Follow these steps in order before you touch a campaign.

**Step 1.** Look for a short-term shock in the last 7 days. For example: your best seller is out of stock, or it is the slow week after a sale. Or a rival is having a sale. It could also be the season, tracking that stopped counting sales, or your site being down.

**Expected result:** You have either found a shock (fix it or wait it out) or ruled one out.

**Step 2.** Look at RPV (revenue per visit: net sales divided by sessions) on the Dashboard. A common rule of thumb: $2.50 to $4 is healthy. Below $2 is hard work, and below $1 makes ads very hard to run at a profit.

**Expected result:** You know whether RPV is healthy.

**Watch out:** Fashion stores often have lower RPV with more visitors. So compare with your own last 90 days too.

<!-- do:metrics -->

**Step 3.** If RPV is low, split it in two. Check if AOV is low or if conversion rate (orders divided by sessions) is low. Then work on the site or offer (Modules 3 and 9), not the ads.

**Expected result:** You have named the weak half of RPV.

**Step 4.** If each visit earns enough, look at clicks next.

1. In Meta Ads Manager, find the **Outbound CTR** column. CTR (click-through rate) is how many people out of 100 who see the ad click to your site.
2. As a rough guide, below 0.5% means the message or the ad itself is weak.

**Expected result:** You know whether the ad is getting clicks.

**Step 5.** Compare cost per click with RPV. If a click costs more than a visit earns, those ads lose money.

**Expected result:** You know whether clicks are affordable.

**Step 6.** Check frequency: how many times, on average, each person saw the ad over the dates you picked (for example the last 7 days). Look for ads with lots of likes and clicks but no sales.

**Expected result:** You have found tired ads (high frequency). Or you found a page that does not match what the ad promised (clicks but no sales).

## Lesson 2.6: When several KPIs are high at once
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** A KPI (key performance indicator) is one of the key numbers you track. When several are bad at once, trying to fix them all spreads you too thin. Fix the one worth the most dollars first.

**Step 1.** For each bad number, imagine it went back to normal. Guess how much more profit you would make a month. For example: MER from 38% to 30% on $40,000 sales = $3,200.

**Expected result:** You have a dollar figure next to each bad number.

**Step 2.** Pick the one with the biggest dollar number. Write it in your decision log as this month's focus.

**Expected result:** You have one focus, usually MER (fast to change) or VCR (the biggest lasting effect).

## Lesson 2.7: The scale test
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Scaling means spending more on ads to get more sales. Done as a planned test with a worst case decided up front, it is safe. Done as a leap, it is how stores burn cash.

**Step 1.** Write down your spend steps, for example $300 a day, then $600, then $900.

**Expected result:** You have three planned spend levels.

**Step 2.** Next to each step, write the MER you expect (total ad spend as a share of total sales). Make it a few points higher each time, for example 28%, 31%, 34%.

**Expected result:** You have expected results that allow for each extra sale costing a bit more.

**Step 3.** Work out the sales needed to break even at each step. If break-even needs more than double your current sales, stop and fix costs first (Lessons 2.3 and 2.4).

**Expected result:** You know the test is worth running.

**Step 4.** Decide your worst case: the most extra ad spend you are willing to lose, and write it down.

**Expected result:** You have a dollar limit that ends the test.

**Step 5.** Make sure you have at least 3 to 5 new ads ready. Then raise to the first step and hold it for 10 to 14 days.

**Expected result:** MER usually gets worse at first, then settles. If it settles, move to the next step; if not, go back one step and find out why.

**Watch out:** A big budget jump can send a Meta ad set back into the learning phase. Many advertisers raise by about 20% at a time. For bigger jumps, test the new budget in a separate campaign (see [Meta: about the learning phase](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/112167992830700)).

## Example

A store makes $50,000 a month. MER is 20%, VCR 40%, FCR 30%, and profit 10%.

1. The store doubles sales to $100,000. Fixed costs stay the same in dollars, so FCR drops to 15%.
2. While growing, MER rises to 30%. That is normal.
3. New profit = 100 - 30 (MER) - 40 (VCR) - 15 (FCR) = 15%.

**What it shows:** the store's problem was heavy fixed costs. Growth was the fix, even though ads got less efficient.

## Lesson 2.8: Why each extra ad dollar buys a bit less
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Your first $100 of ads reaches the people most likely to buy. Each extra $100 reaches people who are a little less keen. So cost per order creeps up as you spend more. Know where that line is, and you can grow without losing money.

**Step 1.** Open your scorecard. Write down your weekly ad spend and new orders for the last 8 weeks.

**Expected result:** A small table with 8 rows of spend and orders.

**Step 2.** Sort the weeks from lowest to highest spend. Work out cost per order for each week (spend divided by orders).

**Expected result:** You can see whether cost per order rises as spend goes up.

**Step 3.** Divide the extra spend in your two biggest weeks by the extra orders they brought, compared with your two smallest weeks.

**Expected result:** One number: the cost of the extra orders. For example, an extra $600 for 10 extra orders is $60 each.

**Step 4.** Compare that number with your break-even CPA, the most you can pay for one order (Lesson 1.5). If it is higher, you are past your safe spend for now.

**Expected result:** You know if your next dollar makes or loses money.

**Step 5.** Raise spend by about 20% a week. Stop when the extra orders cost more than break-even.

**Expected result:** Spend grows only while each step still pays for itself. To grow past that point, make new ads or a better offer first.

**Watch out:** Average cost per order can look fine while the last orders lose money. Always check the extra orders, not just the average.

## Self-check

1. Which kind of constraint do many stores have, even when they blame the ads?
2. Name three fixes for high shipping cost.
3. RPV is $3.20 and MER is high. Is it the site or the ads?
4. Outbound CTR is 0.4%. What do you change?
5. What is the most you can lose in a scale test?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Not enough sales (throughput).
2. Ask for better carrier rates, use boxes that fit, raise the free shipping amount. Also: charge for express, or use a warehouse closer to your customers.
3. The ads. An RPV of $3.20 is healthy.
4. The ad itself: the picture or video, the message, or the offer.
5. The extra ad spend.
