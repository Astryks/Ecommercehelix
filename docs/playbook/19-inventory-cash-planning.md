# Module 19: Inventory, cash and the year plan

**Outcome:** the right stock at the right time, and a cash plan you trust. Plus a plan for the year that ties sales, stock and marketing together.

**Related SOP:** [17 Inventory and cash planning](../sops/17-inventory-cash-planning.md)


<!-- plain:start -->
> **In plain words**
>
> **Stage:** Grow (keep more of every sale, then scale). Every lesson below is tagged with its stage; see [Attract, Convert, Grow](stages.md).
>
> **What it is:** How to keep the right amount of stock and enough cash in the bank.
>
> **Why it matters:** Running out of stock stops sales. Too much stock ties up cash you need for ads.
>
> **Do this:**
>
> 1. Check how many weeks of stock you have for your best sellers.
> 2. Order before you drop below your safe level.
> 3. Keep a simple 13-week cash forecast and update it weekly.
>
> **Words to know:**
>
> - **Weeks of cover:** How many weeks your stock will last at the current sales speed.
> - **Cash forecast:** A week-by-week list of money coming in and going out, so you can see tight weeks early.
>
> All words are explained in the [glossary](../glossary.md).

## Lesson 19.1: Range planning
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Your range is the set of products you sell. Planning it on purpose puts your cash into styles that earn, and lets you test new ideas small before committing.

**Step 1.** Choose the categories that earn the most and fit your brand. Use last year's sales and margin by category.

**Expected result:** You have a short list of focus categories.

**Step 2.** Track every style in one table. Note the launch date, units sold, sell-through % (share of stock sold), margin and whether you will reorder.

**Expected result:** Every style has a row you update monthly.

<!-- do:stock -->

**Step 3.** Order small first runs of new styles and reorder winners quickly.

**Expected result:** New styles are tested with little cash at risk.

**Step 4.** Write a range plan: styles per category and price points. Note how many are new and how many are core (always in stock).

**Expected result:** You have a one-page range plan.

**Step 5.** Forecast core products from their sales history and new products from similar past launches.

**Expected result:** Each product has a forecast based on evidence.

## Lesson 19.2: A 10-minute weekly stock check
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** A weekly stock check takes ten minutes. It stops two costly mistakes: selling out of a best seller, and sitting on stock that will not sell. [SOP 17](../sops/17-inventory-cash-planning.md) has the full steps.

**Step 1.** Open **Suppliers & stock** in Helix and update on-hand and on-order counts for each product.

**Expected result:** Counts are current for this week.

<!-- do:stock -->

**Step 2.** Read weeks of cover (stock divided by units sold per week) for each product.

**Expected result:** You know how long each product will last.

**Step 3.** Grade products A, B or C. A items are the few that make most of your sales. B is the middle, and C is slow sellers.

**Expected result:** Every product has a grade.

**Step 4.** Note reorder dates. Flag products with too little stock (about to sell out) or too much (cash stuck on shelves).

**Expected result:** You have a short list of products to reorder and products to clear.

**Watch out:** Do not run ads hard on a product that is about to sell out.

## Lesson 19.3: Five cash levers
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Stock ties up cash, and cash is what lets you grow. Five levers free it up: buy cheaper, get better terms, pay later, lower the risk and sell faster.

**Step 1.** Read the five levers in the table below and pick the one easiest to pull this month.

**Expected result:** You have one lever chosen.

**Step 2.** Take one action on it, for example asking your supplier for 30-day terms on the balance.

**Expected result:** You have sent the request or made the change.

### Good to know

| Lever | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Get it cheaper | Better prices on bigger orders, other suppliers, cheaper freight (sea instead of air), packaging changes |
| Get better terms from suppliers | Smaller deposits, production in stages, more time to pay, consignment (pay only when it sells) |
| Pay for it later | Trade finance, supplier credit, inventory loans. Always compare the true cost. |
| Lower the risk | Smaller first orders, preorders, extra safety stock only for A items |
| Sell it faster | Promote products with too much cover, bundles, free gifts, planned clearance (Lesson 19.10) |

## Lesson 19.4: Cash flow forecast
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** A cash flow forecast shows how much cash you will have each week. Profit is not the same as cash in the bank. Stock deposits can drain the account months before the sales arrive.

**Step 1.** Make a spreadsheet with one column per week for the next 13 weeks. Add monthly columns beyond that for big stock buys.

**Expected result:** You have a blank forecast.

**Step 2.** For each week, add rows for starting cash, sales money and stock payments (deposits and balances). Add ad spend, fixed costs, tax and loan repayments too.

**Expected result:** Every week shows an ending cash balance, and you can see the lowest week.

**Step 3.** Put aside a share of profit for tax each month (ask your accountant how much).

**Expected result:** Tax money sits in a separate account.

**Step 4.** Update the forecast at the start of each week.

**Expected result:** A weekly reminder is set and the forecast stays current.

## Lesson 19.5: Should you borrow? Weighing up a loan
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Debt can speed up growth or sink a store. Borrow only when both tests below are true.

**Step 1.** Check the money will clearly earn more than it costs. For example: stock for a product that already sells well, or extra stock for the busy season.

**Expected result:** You can name the return the money will make.

**Step 2.** Check your cash forecast shows you could still make the repayments in a bad month.

**Expected result:** Repayments fit even in your worst month.

**Watch out:** Never borrow to cover losses.

## Lesson 19.6: Money mindset and finance help
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Good money habits make the numbers easy to see and the business easy to run. Four habits cover most of it.

**Step 1.** If you do not have one, open a separate business bank account. Move all business money in and out through it.

**Expected result:** Personal and business money never mix.

**Step 2.** Pay yourself a set amount on the same day each month.

**Expected result:** Your wage is predictable and counted in fixed costs.

**Step 3.** Book 15 minutes a week to look at your numbers in Helix.

**Expected result:** A weekly money check is in your calendar.

<!-- do:growth -->

**Step 4.** Once sales are bigger, or before raising money, consider a part-time finance expert (a fractional CFO).

**Expected result:** You know when outside finance help makes sense.

## Lesson 19.7: The year plan
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** The year plan ties sales, stock and marketing together. Working backwards from monthly targets tells you what to buy and spend, and when.

**Step 1.** Find your growth rate. Compare the last 12 months of sales with the 12 months before (Dashboard, **Year** view).

**Expected result:** You have a growth percentage.

<!-- do:growth -->

**Step 2.** Set a sales target for each month, including your big sales peaks.

**Expected result:** Twelve monthly targets are written down.

<!-- do:goals -->

**Step 3.** Work backwards from each month's target to the stock you need to buy and the marketing budget.

**Expected result:** Each month has a stock order and an ad budget.

**Step 4.** Make a marketing calendar of launches, sales, events and content themes, and review the plan every 6 months.

**Expected result:** The calendar is filled and a 6-month review is booked.

<!-- do:calendar -->

## Lesson 19.8: Finding and vetting suppliers
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** A good supplier makes a good product on time at a fair price. They also tell you early when something goes wrong. Changing supplier mid-season is slow and risky, so vet carefully up front. Helix keeps suppliers, minimums, lead times and terms on the Suppliers & stock page.

**Step 1.** Write a one-page spec: materials, size, finish, packaging, certifications and your target landed cost.

**Expected result:** Every supplier can quote against the same spec.

**Step 2.** Search supplier marketplaces, trade shows and industry directories, ask other founders for introductions, and shortlist 3 to 5.

**Expected result:** You have 3 to 5 candidates. Local makers cost more per unit but have shorter lead times and smaller minimums.

**Step 3.** Ask each one for their business licence, and check it covers your product. Ask for a live video tour of the factory floor, or pay an inspection company to visit.

**Expected result:** You know if each one is a factory (makes the product) or a trading company (buys from factories). Both are checked.

**Step 4.** Order paid samples from at least 2 suppliers. Test them against your spec as a customer would, for 1 to 2 weeks.

**Expected result:** You have a winner and can approve a signed "golden sample" that production must match.

**Step 5.** Ask what sets the MOQ (minimum order quantity). Lower it with fewer colours, a stock fabric or a higher price per unit on the first run.

**Expected result:** Your first order covers no more than 3 to 4 months of expected sales.

**Watch out:** Cash tied up in slow stock is a common way growing stores run out of money.

**Step 6.** Get prices on the same terms, for example FOB (price with goods loaded at the port). Work out landed cost: unit price plus freight, duty and other costs like packaging, inspection, bank fees and broker.

**Expected result:** Each supplier has a comparable landed cost, which you enter as product cost in Helix.

<!-- do:stock -->

**Step 7.** Agree how you will pay, using your own written terms. A common first deal is 30% deposit and 70% before shipping. Pay by bank transfer to an account in the company's name.

**Expected result:** Terms are in writing and you confirm any change of bank details by phone.

**Step 8.** Write a quality checklist from the approved sample. Book an inspection before shipping for bigger orders. Count and spot-check every delivery with photos.

**Expected result:** Problems are caught before you pay the balance or list the stock.

**Step 9.** Share a code of conduct: fair pay, safe work, and no forced or child labour. Share your forecast, and review suppliers every year.

**Expected result:** You have a documented, long-term partnership.

**Watch out:** Watch the exchange rate when paying in US dollars. A few cents can change your margin.

## Lesson 19.9: Reorder points, lead times and stock-outs
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Running out of a best seller loses sales and pauses your ads. Ordering too much ties up cash. A reorder point tells you exactly when to order, and Helix does the maths for every product.

**Step 1.** Work out sales speed: average units a day over the last 4 to 8 weeks. Leave out days you were sold out and big sale days.

**Expected result:** You have units per day for each product.

**Step 2.** Find the lead time: days from placing the order with your supplier (usually when you pay the deposit) to stock ready to sell (making, shipping, customs, receiving).

**Expected result:** You have a lead time in days for each product.

**Step 3.** Set safety days: start with 14, more for long sea freight.

**Expected result:** Each product has a buffer.

**Step 4.** In Helix **Suppliers & stock**, enter sales speed, lead time and safety days for each product.

**Expected result:** Helix shows the reorder point and a countdown to each stock-out. The reorder point is sales speed times (lead time plus safety days).

<!-- do:stock -->

**Step 5.** When stock on hand plus on order drops to the reorder point, place an order. Order enough for the next 1 to 3 months of sales, and at least the supplier's minimum.

**Expected result:** An alert shows on Today when it is time to order, and stock arrives before you run out.

**Step 6.** For Black Friday and Christmas, work out the last safe order date. That is the event date, minus lead time, minus about a week to receive and list.

**Expected result:** Peak stock is ordered in time. Peak week can sell several times a normal week, so check last year.

<!-- do:bfcm -->

### Good to know

**Example:** you sell 2 shirts a day, lead time is 50 days and you keep 14 safety days. The reorder point is 2 x 64 = 128 shirts. With 140 on hand and none on order, you have about 6 days before you must order.

## Lesson 19.10: Clear slow stock without cheapening your brand
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Stock that does not sell is cash stuck on a shelf. It also costs money to store. Clearing it in a planned way turns it back into cash, without training everyone to wait for discounts.

**Step 1.** List products with more than 6 months of stock at current sales speed. Helix shows weeks of cover on the stock plan.

**Expected result:** A list of slow products and how much cash is tied up in each.

**Step 2.** Try gentle fixes first: show it higher on the home page, add it to a bundle, or make it a free gift.

**Expected result:** Some slow stock starts selling at full price or inside bundles.

**Step 3.** For what is left, pick one clearance idea. Ideas: a mystery box at a fixed price, a "last chance" page, or "seconds" for slightly faulty items.

**Expected result:** One clear, time-limited way to sell the rest.

**Step 4.** Offer it to your email list first, for a short window. Keep it off your main ads and away from your best sellers.

**Expected result:** The stock sells mostly to existing fans, and new shoppers still see full prices.

**Step 5.** Price so you at least get your cost back, if you can. Stop when the stock is gone or the window ends.

**Expected result:** Cash back in the bank and space for products that sell.

**Step 6.** Note why it was slow: wrong size, colour, timing or too big an order. Use that next time you order (Lesson 19.1).

**Expected result:** Your next order is smaller for that kind of product.

**Watch out:** If you clear stock every month, the real problem is ordering too much. Fix the buying, not the selling.

## Lesson 19.11: Sold out? Pre-orders and back-in-stock lists
<!-- stage:convert -->

**Why this matters:** A sold-out page with no next step loses the sale forever. A back-in-stock alert or a pre-order keeps the shopper. It also tells you how much to order next.

**Step 1.** Install a back-in-stock app, or use the one in your email tool. Turn on a "Notify me" button for sold-out products.

**Expected result:** Sold-out pages show a "Notify me" button instead of a dead end.

**Step 2.** Write a short email for when stock returns. Say it is back, show the product, and add one button.

**Expected result:** People on the list get an email the moment stock arrives.

**Step 3.** For products with a firm delivery date, turn on pre-orders. Show the ship date clearly on the product page and at checkout.

**Expected result:** Shoppers can buy now and know when it will ship.

**Step 4.** Take payment only if you are sure of the date. If you are not sure, collect a waitlist instead.

**Expected result:** You never hold money for stock that might not come.

**Step 5.** Count sign-ups for each product. Add them to your next order size (Lesson 19.9).

**Expected result:** Your next order matches real demand.

**Watch out:** If a pre-order date slips, email buyers at once with the new date and an easy refund. Silence turns into chargebacks. US: ship by the date you state, or within 30 days if you state none. If you will be late, you must offer to cancel with a full refund (see [FTC: Mail Order Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/business-guide-ftcs-mail-internet-or-telephone-order-merchandise-rule)).

## Lesson 19.12: Count the days your cash is stuck
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** From the day you pay a supplier to the day a customer's money reaches your bank, your cash is stuck. The longer it is stuck, the more cash you need to grow. One simple number shows you how long, and what to fix first.

**Step 1.** Find how many days your stock sits before it sells. In Shopify, open **Analytics > Reports > Inventory remaining per product** and read the **Days of inventory remaining** column (on plans that include reports). Or divide the cost of all the stock you hold by the cost of the stock you sell in an average day. For example, $27,000 of stock ÷ $300 sold per day = 90 days.

**Expected result:** One number: days stock sits (for example 90).

**Step 2.** Find how many days it takes for customer money to reach your bank. Check your payouts page. Card payouts often take 2 to 3 business days (see [Shopify: payout timing](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/shopify-payments/payouts/payout-timing)).

**Expected result:** One number: days until you are paid (for example 3).

**Step 3.** Find how many days your supplier gives you to pay. Look at your invoices. If you pay before the goods ship, this is 0.

**Expected result:** One number: days to pay the supplier (for example 30).

**Step 4.** Add them up: days stock sits + days until you are paid − days the supplier gives you. In the example: 90 + 3 − 30 = 63 days your cash is stuck.

**Expected result:** One number for your store, written in your cash plan.

**Step 5.** Pick one lever from Lesson 19.3 to cut 10 days. For example, order smaller amounts more often, ask for 30 more days to pay, take pre-orders, or clear slow stock (Lesson 19.10).

**Expected result:** One action sent or done this week.

**Step 6.** Check the number again every 3 months.

**Expected result:** A reminder is set, and you can see if the number is going down.

<!-- do:calendar -->

**Watch out:** If you pay a deposit before goods are made, start counting from the day you paid the deposit, not the day the goods arrived. Deposits make your cash stuck for longer.

## Lesson 19.13: Plan next month's sales and ad spend
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Most owners decide ad spend day by day, so busy months run short of budget and quiet months waste it. A one-page plan for next month sets your expected sales, how much you will spend to get them, and whether you have the cash. It takes about 30 minutes, once a month.

**Step 1.** Forecast next month's sales. Start from the same month last year, or the average of your last three months if you are new. Then adjust for anything you know: a sale, a launch, a holiday or a product that will be out of stock.

**Expected result:** Three numbers: a low, a likely and a high sales forecast.

**Step 2.** Split the likely number into new and returning customers, using the share from your last 90 days on the Dashboard.

**Expected result:** You know how much must come from new buyers, which is the part ads have to pay for.

<!-- do:numbers -->

**Step 3.** Set the ad budget: likely sales times your target MER (Lesson 1.5). For example, $40,000 of likely sales at a 25% target MER gives a $10,000 budget.

**Expected result:** One monthly ad budget that keeps you inside your target.

**Step 4.** Spread the budget across the weeks. Put more into weeks with a sale or launch, and keep about 10% to 20% for testing new ads (Lesson 7.3).

**Expected result:** A weekly budget you can set in each ad account.

**Step 5.** Check the cash. Add up the ad budget and the stock you must buy for the forecast, and compare it with the cash you will have (Lesson 19.4).

**Expected result:** You know the plan is affordable, or you have trimmed it before the month starts.

**Step 6.** Each Monday, compare real sales and spend with the plan. If sales run well below plan for two weeks, bring spend down to match. Do not chase lost sales with extra spend.

**Expected result:** Spend follows real results, and next month's forecast gets more accurate.

**Watch out:** Your first forecasts will be rough. That is fine. Base them on your own numbers, not on hopes, and keep last month's plan next to this one so you can see how close you got.

## Self-check

1. Name the five cash levers.
2. When is borrowing reasonable?
3. A product sells 3 a day, with a 40-day lead time and 14 safety days. What is its reorder point?
4. Next month's likely sales are $40,000 and your target MER is 25%. What is the ad budget?

2. When the money clearly earns more than it costs and you could make repayments in a bad month. Usually for stock that already sells well.

4. $40,000 × 25% = $10,000.
