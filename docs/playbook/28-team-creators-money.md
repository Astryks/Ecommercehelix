# Module 28: Team, creators and money

**Outcome:** you hire the right first helper on the right terms, pay creators with clear agreements, pay overseas suppliers without losing money on currency or scams, compare funding offers by their true cost, keep tool bills in check, look after yourself, and decide calmly whether Amazon is worth adding.

## Lesson 28.1: Your first hire
<!-- stage:grow -->

> **General information, not tax or legal advice.** Check with a registered tax agent or accountant, and the official sources below.

Your first hire should give you back the hours you spend on work that does not need you. See also [Lesson 16.5](16-founder-team-productivity.md#lesson-165-hiring).

**Do this:**

1. For two weeks, write down every task and how long it took. Mark each one: only I can do it, someone could do it with a checklist, or it should stop.
2. Group the checklist tasks. Customer service, order problems, product uploads and reporting often make a good first role.
3. Write the role as outcomes: for example, "every customer email answered within one business day".
4. Give shortlisted people a small paid trial task that matches the real work.
5. Agree terms in writing: hours or deliverables, rate, notice, confidentiality and that work they create belongs to you.
6. Work out whether the person is an employee or a contractor before they start. It depends on the real working arrangement, not the label.

**Australia:** use the ATO's guide on [employee or independent contractor](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/hiring-and-paying-your-workers/employee-or-independent-contractor/difference-between-employees-and-independent-contractors). Employees need super paid with each payday from 1 July 2026, and some contractors need super too. See [Fair Work: hiring employees](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/).

**US:** the IRS looks at control and independence to decide employee (Form W-2) or contractor (Form 1099). Collect a Form W-9 from contractors before you pay them. See [IRS: independent contractor or employee](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee).

**Self-check:** can you name the 10 hours a week your first hire would take off you? Have you checked employee versus contractor?

## Lesson 28.2: Working well with a virtual assistant
<!-- stage:grow -->

A remote assistant can be a great first hire if the work is written down and access is safe.

**Do this:**

1. Record your screen doing each task once, talking through it. Turn it into a short checklist.
2. Give the assistant their own logins with only the access they need. Never share your own password. See [Lesson 25.1](25-account-safety-agencies.md#lesson-251-lock-down-every-account).
3. Agree working hours that overlap with yours for at least an hour a day.
4. Set three weekly numbers they own, such as reply time, tickets closed and errors.
5. Meet for 15 minutes each week to review the numbers and update checklists.
6. Pay on time, in their currency if you can, through a method you can track.
7. When they leave, remove access the same day.

**Australia and US:** check market rates for the skills and country you hire in, and pay fairly. Good assistants leave quickly when underpaid.

**Self-check:** is every task they do written down? Do they have their own logins?

## Lesson 28.3: Paying creators: agreements, disclosure and tax
<!-- stage:grow -->

> **General information, not tax or legal advice.** Check with a registered tax agent or accountant.

Creators are paid in different ways: free product, a flat fee, commission, or a mix. Clear terms prevent arguments later. For rates and usage, see [Lesson 19.4](19-creators-ugc-and-video-ads.md#lesson-194-deals-rates-and-usage-rights).

**Do this:**

1. Break every quote into parts: making the content, posting it, usage rights (using it in your ads, and for how long), exclusivity and raw footage. Each part has its own price.
2. Put it in writing: what they will make, dates, number of revisions, usage rights, payment date and what happens if a post is late.
3. Gifting is not free. Count the product, shipping and your time when you compare it with paid work.
4. Make sure every paid or gifted post is clearly labelled as an ad.
5. Pay on time and keep invoices.
6. Track each creator's results: sales from their code or link, and how their content performs as an ad.

**Australia:** businesses must not mislead consumers, including through influencer posts, and ad disclosure must be obvious. See [ACCC: influencer testimonials and endorsements](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/social-media-influencer-testimonials.pdf). Creators with an ABN who are registered for GST will add GST to their invoice.

**US:** follow the FTC's disclosure rules. See [FTC: Disclosures 101 for social media influencers](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers). Creators you pay $2,000 or more in 2026 usually need a Form 1099-NEC (see [Lesson 27.6](27-accounting-bookkeeping-tax-time.md#lesson-276-year-end-and-tax-season-in-the-us)).

**Self-check:** does every creator deal have written usage rights and dates? Are all posts labelled?

## Lesson 28.4: Paying overseas suppliers and managing currency
<!-- stage:grow -->

> **General information, not financial advice.** Check with your accountant or bank.

When you pay in another currency, the exchange rate margin can cost more than the bank fee. Invoice scams also target importers.

**Do this:**

1. Compare total cost for one real payment: the rate you get against the mid-market rate, plus fees. Do this with your bank and at least one specialist money transfer provider.
2. Pay in the supplier's currency when that is cheaper than letting them convert.
3. Agree payment terms in writing: deposit, balance timing and what happens if goods are late or faulty. See [Lesson 14.8](14-inventory-cash-planning.md#lesson-148-finding-and-vetting-suppliers).
4. Before any first payment, or any change of bank details, call the supplier on a number you already have to confirm the account. Never trust new bank details sent only by email.
5. Card payments can earn points and give some protection, but check surcharges and your card's foreign transaction fee.
6. If large payments are due months ahead, ask your provider about fixing a rate in advance.

**Australia:** report payment redirection scams at [Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/). **US:** report fraud to the [FTC](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).

**Self-check:** do you know the total cost of your last supplier payment? Do you confirm bank details by phone?

## Lesson 28.5: Funding growth: comparing financing offers
<!-- stage:grow -->

> **General information, not financial advice.** Check with your accountant before you borrow.

Revenue-based financing, platform cash advances and bank loans can fund stock for a proven product. They make things worse when they fund losses. See also [Lesson 14.5](14-inventory-cash-planning.md#lesson-145-the-debt-decision).

**Do this:**

1. Only borrow for something with a clear payback, such as more stock of a product that already sells profitably.
2. For each offer, write down: amount received, total amount repaid, the share of daily sales taken, and the expected time to repay.
3. Work out the true cost: total repaid minus amount received, divided by amount received. Then think about how quickly you repay. A flat fee repaid in 4 months costs far more per year than the same fee over 12 months.
4. Check what happens if sales drop. A share-of-sales repayment slows down, but a fixed weekly repayment does not.
5. Model your cash flow with the repayments included before you sign.
6. Read for personal guarantees, early repayment rules and what the lender can take if you stop paying.

**Australia:** look at government support and grants at [business.gov.au: grants and programs](https://business.gov.au/grants-and-programs). **US:** compare SBA-backed loans at [SBA: loans](https://www.sba.gov/funding-programs/loans).

**Self-check:** do you know the true cost of each offer and what you would buy with it? Can you still pay it back if sales drop by a quarter?

## Lesson 28.6: Keep tool and subscription costs in check
<!-- stage:grow -->

Apps and tools creep up quietly. Many charge by contacts, orders or users, so the bill grows with you even when value does not.

**Do this:**

1. Every quarter, export your card and bank statements and list every subscription with its monthly cost.
2. For each tool, write down what it does for sales or time saved. Cancel anything you cannot justify.
3. Email and SMS tools often charge by the number of contacts you keep. Remove contacts who have not opened or clicked for a long time (after a last win-back message). See [Module 10](10-email-sms-whatsapp.md).
4. Check plan limits before a big sale, so you are not bumped to a higher tier by surprise.
5. Pay yearly only for tools you are certain you will keep.
6. Put total tools cost into your monthly fixed costs in Helix.

**Australia and US:** remember to remove apps from Shopify properly; some leave code or keep billing until uninstalled.

**Self-check:** do you know your total monthly tool spend? When did you last clean your email list?

## Lesson 28.7: Founder load: stress, burnout and getting help
<!-- stage:grow -->

Running a store can be lonely, and money worries can keep you awake. Looking after yourself is part of running the business. See also [Lesson 16.8](16-founder-team-productivity.md#lesson-168-resilience).

**Do this:**

1. Look at your numbers on a schedule: profit each morning in Helix, cash once a week. Regular small check-ins cause less dread than avoiding them.
2. Keep a cash buffer you do not touch, even a small one. Knowing it is there lowers stress.
3. Write down the three things that worry you most, and one small next step for each.
4. Protect sleep, movement and at least one day a week away from the business.
5. Talk to someone: a friend, a peer, a mentor or a professional. You do not have to wait for a crisis.
6. Hand off one draining task this month (see Lesson 28.1).

**Australia:** Beyond Blue (1300 22 4636) and Lifeline (13 11 14) offer free support at any hour. Beyond Blue also runs free coaching for small business owners, and the Small Business Debt Helpline (1800 413 828) helps with money worries. See [business.gov.au: mental health and wellbeing support](https://business.gov.au/risk-management/mental-health/mental-health-and-wellbeing-support-for-business).

**US:** call or text 988 for the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline at any hour. See [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/).

**Self-check:** when did you last take a full day off? Who could you call this week?

## Lesson 28.8: Should you sell on Amazon?
<!-- stage:grow -->

Amazon can add sales, but it has its own fees, rules and competition. Decide with numbers, not fear of missing out.

**Do this:**

1. Search Amazon for your product type. Note prices, review counts and whether copies of your product already exist.
2. Work out profit per unit on Amazon: price minus referral fee, fulfilment fee, storage, ads and returns. Use Amazon's own fee calculator.
3. Decide how you would sell: as a seller (you set prices and manage listings) or, if invited, as a supplier selling wholesale to Amazon.
4. Protect your brand first: register your trademark and enrol in Amazon's brand registry before you list.
5. Start with your best seller only, and keep stock separate so your own store never runs out.
6. Review after 90 days: profit, effect on your own store's sales and time spent.

**Australia:** Amazon's Australian marketplace is smaller than the US one; check demand for your category first. **US:** marketplaces usually collect sales tax on their sales for you (see [Lesson 26.3](26-tax-gst-sales-tax-duties.md#lesson-263-us-sales-tax-nexus-registering-and-filing)).

**Self-check:** do you know your profit per unit on Amazon after all fees? Is your trademark registered?
