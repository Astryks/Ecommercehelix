# Module 21: Fulfilment, shipping and returns

**Outcome:** orders leave on time and you know shipping costs before you set prices. Delivery problems have a plan, and returns cost you less each month. You know when to hand packing to a warehouse partner and how to move without losing orders.

**Related:** [Module 19 Inventory, cash and the year plan](19-inventory-cash-planning.md), [Module 13 Shopify store setup](13-shopify-store-setup.md), [Module 22 Tax, GST, sales tax and duties](22-tax-gst-sales-tax-duties.md), [Module 14 Website, conversion and order value](14-website-and-cro.md)


<!-- plain:start -->
> **In plain words**
>
> **Stage:** Grow (keep more of every sale, then scale). Every lesson below is tagged with its stage; see [Attract, Convert, Grow](stages.md).
>
> **What it is:** How orders get packed, shipped and returned without eating your profit.
>
> **Why it matters:** Shipping and returns are often your biggest costs after the product. Late or lost parcels lose customers for good.
>
> **Do this:**
>
> 1. Work out your true cost per order and get three warehouse quotes when packing takes over your week.
> 2. Know your shipping cost per order, show delivery times and keep a second carrier ready.
> 3. Track your return rate and top return reason, and fix the biggest one first.
>
> **Words to know:**
>
> - **3PL:** Third-party logistics: a warehouse company that stores your stock and packs and ships your orders.
> - **Return rate:** The share of orders that customers send back.
> - **Shipping protection:** An optional fee at checkout that covers lost, stolen or damaged parcels.
> - **Lead time:** How long it takes from ordering from your supplier to having stock ready to sell.
>
> All words are explained in the [glossary](../glossary.md).

## Lesson 21.1: Do you need a 3PL yet?
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Packing orders yourself is cheap at first and expensive later, because your hours stop going into growth. A 3PL is a warehouse company that stores and ships for you. It makes sense when packing takes more of your week than selling does.

**Step 1.** For one normal week, write down how many orders you sent. Note the hours spent packing, labelling and dropping off.

**Expected result:** You have orders per week and packing hours per week.

**Step 2.** Work out your cost per order today. Add packaging, labels, postage, and your time at a fair hourly rate.

**Expected result:** You have one true cost per order, including your time.

**Step 3.** Ask at least 3 warehouses for a price on the same sample month. Give orders per month, units per order, number of products, carton sizes, and pallets or shelves of stock.

**Expected result:** You have 3 quotes for the same workload.

**Watch out:** Outside the US: in Australia, most warehouses are in the biggest cities. Ask for delivery times to Perth and country areas. US: one central warehouse reaches most addresses in about 2 to 5 business days by ground. Stock held in a state can mean sales tax there, so read [Lesson 22.3](22-tax-gst-sales-tax-duties.md#lesson-223-us-sales-tax-nexus-registering-and-filing) first.

**Step 4.** Compare the full monthly bill, not just pick and pack. Include receiving, storage (per pallet, shelf or cubic metre) and packaging. Add monthly minimums, set-up, returns and busy-season extras.

**Expected result:** You can see the real total for each quote.

**Step 5.** Ask each warehouse for a sample invoice from a client of similar size.

**Expected result:** You can see fees the price sheet leaves out.

**Step 6.** Ask 3 service questions. What is the daily order cut-off time? What error rate do they measure? What do they do when they make a mistake?

**Expected result:** You know how reliable each warehouse is.

**Step 7.** Confirm orders flow in from Shopify automatically and tracking flows back.

**Expected result:** The warehouse connects to your store with no manual copying.

## Lesson 21.2: Moving to a 3PL without losing orders
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Most moves to a warehouse go wrong in the handover week. Stock counts do not match, barcodes are missing or orders get stuck between systems. A careful switch avoids all three.

**Step 1.** Give every product and variant its own SKU (your product code) and a barcode. Do it before anything ships (see [Module 13](13-shopify-store-setup.md)).

**Expected result:** Every variant has a SKU and barcode.

**Step 2.** Send a delivery notice with each shipment, listing what is in each carton by SKU and quantity.

**Expected result:** The warehouse knows what is arriving and does not charge extra for unlabelled cartons.

**Step 3.** Ask the warehouse to count everything when it arrives and send you the count. Fix gaps before you switch.

**Expected result:** Their count matches what you sent.

**Step 4.** Pick a quiet day to switch, never the week before a sale.

**Expected result:** You have a switch date away from any promotion.

**Step 5.** Place 3 test orders: one item, several items, and a remote address.

**Expected result:** All 3 arrive and tracking reaches the customer.

**Step 6.** For the first month, match stock every week.

1. Put the warehouse stock report next to Shopify's **Inventory** page.
2. Ask the warehouse about any gap bigger than a few units.

**Expected result:** Stock numbers stay in step.

**Watch out:** Keep a small amount of stock at home for the first month so you can send replacements fast.

**Step 7.** Write down who to call when something goes wrong and how fast they promise to answer.

**Expected result:** You have a named contact and response time.

## Lesson 21.3: Shipping rates, carriers and delays
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Shipping is often one of your biggest costs after the product itself. Know it before you set prices and free shipping rules, and have a plan for delays.

**Step 1.** Weigh and measure your 3 most common parcels.

**Expected result:** You know the size and weight of each. Carriers charge by size as well as weight (cubic or dimensional weight), so smaller boxes can save money. See [Australia Post: cubic weight](https://auspost.com.au/business/grow-your-business/business-ideas-blog/what-is-cubic-weight) and [USPS: size and weight](https://faq.usps.com/articles/Knowledge/Parcel-Size-Weight-Fee-Standards).

**Step 2.** Get rates from at least 2 carriers for your top delivery zones.

**Expected result:** You can compare prices side by side.

**Watch out:** Outside the US: compare the national post with private couriers, especially over 1 kg (in Australia, also for WA and regional areas). US: compare the postal service with private carriers; ground services are often cheaper for heavy parcels and the postal service for small light ones.

**Step 3.** Work out your average shipping cost per order from last month's bills. Enter it in Helix under shipping.

**Expected result:** Helix shows true profit including shipping.

<!-- do:numbers -->

**Step 4.** Choose a rule: free shipping over an amount, a flat rate, or live rates. Set any free shipping amount a little above your average order (see [Lesson 14.10](14-website-and-cro.md#lesson-1410-shipping-psychology)).

**Expected result:** You have one clear shipping rule.

**Step 5.** Show delivery times on product pages and at checkout.

**Expected result:** Fewer "where is my order" emails.

**Step 6.** Write a plan for delivery problems. Have a second carrier you can switch to, an email you can send in minutes, and a store banner.

**Expected result:** You can react to a postal delay the same day.

**Watch out:** US: ship within the time you state, or 30 days if you state none. If you will be late, tell buyers and offer to cancel with a full refund (see [FTC: Mail Order Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/business-guide-ftcs-mail-internet-or-telephone-order-merchandise-rule)).

**Step 7.** Review carrier prices each time they announce a rise, and at least once a year.

**Expected result:** Your shipping rates never fall behind your costs.

## Lesson 21.4: Shipping protection and lost or damaged parcels
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Some parcels will go missing or arrive broken. Decide early who pays and how fast you fix it. Also decide if you offer shipping protection: a small fee the buyer can choose that covers loss or damage.

**Step 1.** Track lost and damaged parcels for one month: how many, what they cost and which carrier.

**Expected result:** You know the monthly cost of lost and damaged parcels.

**Step 2.** Write a simple rule. For example: resend or refund once the carrier marks a parcel lost, or the customer sends a photo of damage.

**Expected result:** Anyone on your team can handle a claim the same way.

**Step 3.** Claim from the carrier when you can, keeping proof of value and photos of packaging.

**Expected result:** Some of the cost comes back.

**Step 4.** If you offer shipping protection, let buyers choose it. Leave it unticked, with the price shown (see [Shopify app store requirements](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/shopify-app-store/app-store-requirements)).

**Expected result:** Customers choose it; it is never pre-selected. Shopify's app rules ban apps from pre-selecting optional charges.

**Watch out:** Outside the US: in Australia, pre-selected extras must be included in the total price you show (see [ACCC: price displays](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/pricing/price-displays)). US: the [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing) treats hidden or pre-checked charges as a risk area.

**Step 5.** Record protection fees as income and resends as costs.

**Expected result:** You can see whether protection pays for itself.

**Step 6.** Improve the packaging for anything that breaks more than once.

**Expected result:** Damage claims fall.

## Lesson 21.5: Returns and exchanges that cost less
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Every return costs you twice: the refund and the handling. The aim is fewer returns and more exchanges, while always meeting the law.

**Step 1.** Work out your return rate: returned orders divided by orders for the last 3 months, by product.

**Expected result:** You have a return rate per product.

**Step 2.** Record a reason for every return: too small, too big, not as pictured, faulty or changed mind. Fix the top reason first.

**Expected result:** You know your number one return reason.

**Step 3.** Cut returns at the source with a clear size guide, real photos, measurements and reviews that mention fit.

**Expected result:** Product pages answer fit and look questions.

**Step 4.** Offer an exchange or store credit first, with a refund still available where the law requires it.

**Expected result:** More returns become exchanges.

**Watch out:** Outside the US: in Australia, customers can get a repair, replacement or refund for faulty products. That is true whatever your policy says. Never write "no refunds"; "no refunds for change of mind" is fine (see [ACCC](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/buying-products-and-services/consumer-rights-and-guarantees)). UK and EU: online shoppers can usually cancel within 14 days of delivery without giving a reason, so "no change of mind refunds" does not work for them (see [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/online-and-distance-selling-for-businesses) and [Your Europe](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/consumers/shopping/returns/index_en.htm)). EU: since 19 June 2026, if people buy on your website, you must also give them an online withdrawal function, labelled "withdraw from contract here" or something just as clear, that works for the whole 14 days. Confirm each withdrawal by email straight away (see [EUR-Lex: Directive (EU) 2023/2673](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32023L2673)). US: honour the policy you publish; some states have their own rules (see the [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance)).

**Step 5.** Make returns easy to start with a form or portal, checking the portal's price against your return volume.

**Expected result:** Returns are simple and the tool pays for itself.

**Step 6.** Inspect returned items quickly and put sellable stock back on sale.

**Expected result:** Returned stock is sellable again within days.

**Step 7.** Enter refunds in Helix.

**Expected result:** Your profit numbers include refunds.

## Lesson 21.6: When a spreadsheet stops working for stock
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** A spreadsheet works for a handful of products. It breaks when you have many variants, more than one location, or purchase orders in transit.

**Step 1.** Start with the Helix [Suppliers and stock](/dashboard/stock) page. It shows reorder points, stock-out dates and order-by dates (see [Lesson 19.9](19-inventory-cash-planning.md#lesson-199-reorder-points-lead-times-and-stock-outs)).

**Expected result:** Each product shows when to reorder.

<!-- do:stock -->

**Step 2.** Move to stock software when 2 or more of these are true. You have over about 100 variants or more than one warehouse. Or you make stock mistakes often, or spend hours each week on stock.

**Expected result:** You know whether you need software yet.

**Step 3.** Before you buy, list what you need. For example: purchase orders and landed cost (product plus freight, duties and fees). Or several locations, bundles and a link to your accounts.

**Expected result:** You have a short requirements list.

**Step 4.** Trial the tool with your own data and check bundles and returns work.

**Expected result:** The tool handles your real cases.

**Step 5.** Count all stock at least once a year (see [Lesson 23.3](23-accounting-bookkeeping-tax-time.md#lesson-233-cost-of-goods-and-stock-in-your-books)) and spot-check best sellers monthly.

**Expected result:** There is one source of truth for stock and it is accurate.

## Lesson 21.7: Customer service that keeps customers
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Most questions are the same few. Where is my order? Can I change my size? How do I return this? Fast, friendly answers turn a worried buyer into a repeat buyer. Good automation takes most of the work off you.

**Step 1.** Read your last 50 customer emails and messages and group them by question. Count the top five.

**Expected result:** A list of your five most common questions, with how often each comes up.

**Step 2.** Write a saved reply for each of the five in your own voice. Put the answers on your FAQ and order pages, so fewer people need to ask.

**Expected result:** Five saved replies, and the same answers on your site.

**Step 3.** Bring email, chat and social messages into one shared inbox, usually a help desk app. Switch on an automatic order tracking reply.

**Expected result:** Every message lands in one place, and "where is my order" gets an instant answer. Shopify Inbox covers chat and social messages, not email.

**Step 4.** Set a reply target, like within one business day. Check each week how many messages missed it.

**Expected result:** You know your reply time and which questions still take the longest.

**Watch out:** Never argue in public replies. Move complaints to private messages quickly, fix the problem, then thank the customer publicly.

## Lesson 21.8: Product safety, labelling and recalls
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Some products must meet safety rules before you can sell them, and a few are banned outright. If a product hurts someone, you have days, not weeks, to tell the regulator. Fines for unsafe or unlabelled products can be far bigger than the profit on them. A one-hour check per product, and a plan written before anything goes wrong, keeps you safe.

**Step 1.** List every product and the countries you sell it in. For Australia, search the mandatory standards and bans on [ACCC Product Safety](https://www.productsafety.gov.au/business/search-mandatory-standards) for each product type.

**Expected result:** Each product is marked "standard applies", "banned" or "nothing found", with the date you checked.

**Watch out:** Take extra care with products that contain button or coin batteries, toys for young children, infant sleep products and furniture that can tip over. Sellers must meet mandatory standards and must not sell banned products, even if a supplier made them (see [ACCC: product safety responsibilities](https://www.accc.gov.au/business/selling-products-and-services/product-safety-responsibilities)). Products with button or coin batteries need warnings on the packaging and in the instructions (or a swing tag if unpackaged), and the ACCC recommends the same warning in your product listing (see [ACCC Product Safety: button and coin battery information standard](https://www.productsafety.gov.au/business/search-mandatory-standards/button-and-coin-batteries-mandatory-standards/products-containing-button-and-coin-batteries-mandatory-information-standard)). Not sure which standard applies? The ACCC suggests getting legal advice.

**Step 2.** Ask your supplier for proof for every product that a standard covers: test reports against that standard, the labels and the instructions. Save them in one folder per product.

**Expected result:** Each covered product has a test report, label and instructions on file before you sell it.

**Step 3.** Check every label and listing. Warnings, age grading and instructions must match the product and be easy to read. Put the same safety warnings in the product description.

**Expected result:** What the customer reads online matches what arrives in the box.

**Step 4.** Selling to the EU? Add the details the General Product Safety Regulation (GPSR) asks for to every listing. It has applied since 13 December 2024.

**Expected result:** Every EU listing clearly shows four things: the manufacturer's name, postal address and email address; if the manufacturer is outside the EU, the same details for the **EU responsible person**; a picture of the product, its type and any other identifier (such as a model or batch number); and any warnings or safety information, in a language the customer understands. If you cannot name an EU responsible person, pause EU sales of that product.

**Watch out:** The GPSR says a product cannot be placed on the EU market unless an economic operator based in the EU is responsible for it. The product itself must also carry a type, batch or serial number and the manufacturer's contact details (see [EUR-Lex: Regulation (EU) 2023/988](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32023R0988), Articles 9, 16 and 19).

**Step 5.** Selling to the US? Check whether a Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) rule covers your product. Children's products (made mainly for children 12 or under) covered by a children's product safety rule need testing by a CPSC-accepted lab and a written **Children's Product Certificate**. Other covered products use a General Certificate of Conformity.

**Expected result:** Each covered product has its certificate and test reports on file, in English.

**Watch out:** Since 8 July 2026, importers of most regulated consumer products must file certificate data electronically with US Customs (see [CPSC: Children's Product Certificate](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Testing-Certification/Childrens-Product-Certificate)). If your supplier or freight forwarder is the importer, ask who files it.

**Step 6.** Write a one-page incident plan. Any message that mentions an injury, burn, choking, rash or illness goes to one person the same day. That person checks the reporting deadlines in the table below and reports if needed.

**Expected result:** A short plan with one owner, the deadlines and the report links.

**Watch out:** In Australia, you must report to the ACCC within 2 days of becoming aware that a product you supplied caused, or may have caused, a death, serious injury or serious illness. This applies even if the product was misused in a way you could foresee, and it covers retailers, not just makers. If in doubt, report: a mandatory report is confidential and not an admission of liability (see [ACCC Product Safety: when you must report](https://www.productsafety.gov.au/business/make-a-mandatory-report/when-you-must-report-an-incident)).

**Step 7.** If a product is unsafe, act straight away. Stop selling it (unpublish it or set stock to zero), tell the regulator, contact every buyer using your order emails, and offer a refund, repair or replacement. Keep a record of every step.

**Expected result:** Nobody else can buy the product, buyers know what to do, and the regulator has been told on time.

**Watch out:** In Australia, if you recall a product for safety reasons, tell the ACCC within 2 days of taking recall action (see [ACCC Product Safety: tell the ACCC of the recall](https://www.productsafety.gov.au/business/recall-an-unsafe-product/tell-the-accc-of-the-recall)). Sellers must give a refund or replacement for unsafe products and must not send the customer to the manufacturer.

### Good to know

| Where | Report what | Deadline | Where to report |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Australia | A death, serious injury or serious illness a product caused or may have caused | Within 2 days of becoming aware | [ACCC Product Safety](https://www.productsafety.gov.au/business/make-a-mandatory-report/when-you-must-report-an-incident) |
| Australia | A safety recall you start | Within 2 days of taking recall action | [Recall notification](https://www.productsafety.gov.au/business/recall-an-unsafe-product/tell-the-accc-of-the-recall) |
| EU | An accident caused by a product (the manufacturer reports) | Without undue delay | Safety Business Gateway (GPSR Article 20) |
| US | A possible defect or hazard, or a product that breaks a CPSC rule | Within 24 hours of having the information | [CPSC: duty to report](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Recall-Guidance/Duty-to-Report-to-the-CPSC-Your-Rights-and-Responsibilities) |

The CPSC's advice is "when in doubt, report", and a US report does not have to wait for an injury.

> **General information, not legal advice.** Rules change and depend on the product. Check the official pages above, and ask a lawyer or a testing lab when a product is for children, uses batteries, or plugs in.
