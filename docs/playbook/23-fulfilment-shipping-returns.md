# Module 23: Fulfilment, shipping and returns

**Outcome:** orders leave on time, shipping costs are known before you set prices, delivery problems have a plan, and returns cost you less each month. You know when to hand packing to a warehouse partner and how to move without losing orders.

**Related:** [Module 14 Inventory, cash and the year plan](14-inventory-cash-planning.md), [Module 22 Shopify store setup](22-shopify-store-setup.md), [Module 26 Tax, GST, sales tax and duties](26-tax-gst-sales-tax-duties.md), [Module 9 Website, conversion and order value](09-website-and-cro.md)

## Lesson 23.1: Do you need a 3PL yet?
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Packing orders yourself is cheap at first and expensive later, because your hours stop going into growth. A 3PL (third party logistics: a warehouse company that stores and ships for you) makes sense when packing takes more of your week than selling does.

**Step 1.** For one normal week, write down how many orders you sent and how many hours packing, labelling and drop-offs took.

**Expected result:** You have orders per week and packing hours per week.

**Step 2.** Work out your cost per order today: packaging plus labels plus postage plus your time at a fair hourly rate.

**Expected result:** You have one true cost per order, including your time.

**Step 3.** Ask at least 3 warehouses to quote on the same sample month: orders per month, units per order, number of products, carton sizes and pallets or shelves of stock.

**Expected result:** You have 3 quotes for the same workload.

**Watch out:** Australia: most warehouses are in Sydney, Melbourne or Brisbane, so ask for delivery times to Perth and regional areas. US: one central warehouse reaches most addresses in 2 to 5 days by ground; stock held in a state can create sales tax duties there, so read [Lesson 26.3](26-tax-gst-sales-tax-duties.md#lesson-263-us-sales-tax-nexus-registering-and-filing) first.

**Step 4.** Compare the full monthly bill, not just pick and pack: receiving, storage (per pallet, shelf or cubic metre), packaging, monthly minimums, set-up, returns handling and peak surcharges.

**Expected result:** You can see the real total for each quote.

**Step 5.** Ask each warehouse for a sample invoice from a client of similar size.

**Expected result:** You can see fees the price sheet leaves out.

**Step 6.** Ask 3 service questions: the daily order cut-off time, the error rate they measure, and what they do when they make a mistake.

**Expected result:** You know how reliable each warehouse is.

**Step 7.** Confirm orders flow in from Shopify automatically and tracking flows back.

**Expected result:** The warehouse connects to your store with no manual copying.

## Lesson 23.2: Moving to a 3PL without losing orders
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Most moves to a warehouse go wrong in the handover week: stock counts do not match, barcodes are missing or orders get stuck between systems. A careful switch avoids all three.

**Step 1.** Give every product and variant a unique SKU (stock keeping unit: your own product code) and a barcode before anything ships (see [Module 22](22-shopify-store-setup.md)).

**Expected result:** Every variant has a SKU and barcode.

**Step 2.** Send a delivery notice with each shipment, listing what is in each carton by SKU and quantity.

**Expected result:** The warehouse knows what is arriving and does not charge extra for unlabelled cartons.

**Step 3.** Ask the warehouse to count everything on arrival and send you the count, then fix gaps before you switch.

**Expected result:** Their count matches what you sent.

**Step 4.** Pick a quiet day to switch, never the week before a sale.

**Expected result:** You have a switch date away from any promotion.

**Step 5.** Place 3 test orders: one item, several items, and a remote address.

**Expected result:** All 3 arrive and tracking reaches the customer.

**Step 6.** For the first month, compare the warehouse stock report with Shopify every week and check any gap bigger than a few units.

**Expected result:** Stock numbers stay in step.

**Watch out:** Keep a small amount of stock at home for the first month so you can send replacements fast.

**Step 7.** Write down who to call when something goes wrong and how fast they promise to answer.

**Expected result:** You have a named contact and response time.

## Lesson 23.3: Shipping rates, carriers and delays
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Shipping is often your second biggest cost after the product itself. Know it before you set prices and free shipping rules, and have a plan for delays.

**Step 1.** Weigh and measure your 3 most common parcels.

**Expected result:** You know the size and weight of each. Carriers charge by size as well as weight (cubic or dimensional weight), so smaller boxes can save money.

**Step 2.** Get rates from at least 2 carriers for your top delivery zones.

**Expected result:** You can compare prices side by side.

**Watch out:** Australia: compare the national post with private couriers, especially over 1 kg and for WA and regional areas. US: compare USPS, UPS and FedEx; ground is often cheaper for heavy parcels and USPS for small light ones.

**Step 3.** Work out your average shipping cost per order from last month's invoices and enter it in Helix under shipping.

**Expected result:** Helix shows true profit including shipping.

<!-- do:numbers -->

**Step 4.** Choose a rule: free shipping over an amount, a flat rate, or real-time rates, with any free shipping amount a little above your average order (see [Lesson 9.11](09-website-and-cro.md#lesson-911-shipping-psychology)).

**Expected result:** You have one clear shipping rule.

**Step 5.** Show delivery times on product pages and at checkout.

**Expected result:** Fewer "where is my order" emails.

**Step 6.** Write a disruption plan: a second carrier you can switch to, an email you can send in minutes and a store banner.

**Expected result:** You can react to a postal delay the same day.

**Step 7.** Review carrier prices each time they announce a rise, and at least once a year.

**Expected result:** Your shipping rates never fall behind your costs.

## Lesson 23.4: Shipping protection and lost or damaged parcels
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Some parcels will go missing or arrive broken. Decide in advance who pays, how fast you fix it and whether you offer optional shipping protection (a small opt-in fee that covers loss or damage).

**Step 1.** Track lost and damaged parcels for one month: how many, what they cost and which carrier.

**Expected result:** You know the monthly cost of lost and damaged parcels.

**Step 2.** Write a simple rule, such as: resend or refund once the carrier marks a parcel lost or the customer sends a photo of damage.

**Expected result:** Anyone on your team can handle a claim the same way.

**Step 3.** Claim from the carrier when you can, keeping proof of value and photos of packaging.

**Expected result:** Some of the cost comes back.

**Step 4.** If you offer shipping protection, make it opt-in: unticked by default with the price shown (see [Shopify app store requirements](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/shopify-app-store/app-store-requirements)).

**Expected result:** Customers choose it; it is never pre-selected.

**Watch out:** Australia: pre-selected extras can count as part of the headline price (see [ACCC: price displays](https://www.accc.gov.au/business/pricing/price-displays)). US: the [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing) treats hidden or pre-checked charges as a risk area.

**Step 5.** Record protection fees as income and resends as costs.

**Expected result:** You can see whether protection pays for itself.

**Step 6.** Improve the packaging for anything that breaks more than once.

**Expected result:** Damage claims fall.

## Lesson 23.5: Returns and exchanges that cost less
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** Every return costs you twice: the refund and the handling. The aim is fewer returns and more exchanges, while always meeting the law.

**Step 1.** Work out your return rate: returned orders divided by orders for the last 3 months, by product.

**Expected result:** You have a return rate per product.

**Step 2.** Record a reason for every return (too small, too big, not as pictured, faulty, changed mind) and fix the top reason first.

**Expected result:** You know your number one return reason.

**Step 3.** Cut returns at the source with a clear size guide, real photos, measurements and reviews that mention fit.

**Expected result:** Product pages answer fit and look questions.

**Step 4.** Offer an exchange or store credit first, with a refund still available where the law requires it.

**Expected result:** More returns become exchanges.

**Watch out:** Australia: customers have rights to repair, replacement or refund for faulty products whatever your policy says; never write "no refunds" (see [ACCC](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/buying-products-and-services/consumer-rights-and-guarantees)). US: honour the policy you publish; some states have their own rules (see the [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance)).

**Step 5.** Make returns easy to start with a form or portal, checking the portal's price against your return volume.

**Expected result:** Returns are simple and the tool pays for itself.

**Step 6.** Inspect returned items quickly and put sellable stock back on sale.

**Expected result:** Returned stock is sellable again within days.

**Step 7.** Enter refunds in Helix.

**Expected result:** Your profit numbers include refunds.

## Lesson 23.6: When a spreadsheet stops working for stock
<!-- stage:grow -->

**Why this matters:** A spreadsheet works for a handful of products. It breaks when you have many variants, more than one location, or purchase orders in transit.

**Step 1.** Start with the Helix [Suppliers and stock](/dashboard/stock) page for reorder points, stock-out dates and order-by dates (see [Lesson 14.9](14-inventory-cash-planning.md)).

**Expected result:** Each product shows when to reorder.

<!-- do:stock -->

**Step 2.** Move to inventory software when 2 or more are true: over about 100 variants, more than one warehouse, frequent stock mistakes, or hours each week spent on stock.

**Expected result:** You know whether you need software yet.

**Step 3.** Before you buy, list what you need: purchase orders, landed cost (product plus freight, duties and fees), multiple locations, bundles and an accounting link.

**Expected result:** You have a short requirements list.

**Step 4.** Trial the tool with your own data and check bundles and returns work.

**Expected result:** The tool handles your real cases.

**Step 5.** Count all stock at least once a year (see [Lesson 27.3](27-accounting-bookkeeping-tax-time.md#lesson-273-cost-of-goods-and-stock-in-your-books)) and spot-check best sellers monthly.

**Expected result:** There is one source of truth for stock and it is accurate.
