# Module 23: Fulfilment, shipping and returns

**Outcome:** orders leave on time, shipping costs are known before you set prices, delivery problems have a plan, and returns cost you less each month. You know when to hand packing to a warehouse partner and how to move without losing orders.

**Related:** [Module 14 Inventory, cash and the year plan](14-inventory-cash-planning.md), [Module 22 Shopify store setup](22-shopify-store-setup.md), [Module 26 Tax, GST, sales tax and duties](26-tax-gst-sales-tax-duties.md), [Module 9 Website, conversion and order value](09-website-and-cro.md)

> **In plain words**
>
> **What it is:** How orders get packed, shipped and returned without eating your profit.
>
> **Why it matters:** Shipping and returns are often your biggest costs after the product. Late or lost parcels lose customers for good.
>
> **Do this:**
>
> 1. Work out your true cost per order and get three warehouse quotes when packing takes over your week.
> 2. Know your shipping cost per order, show delivery times and keep a second carrier ready.
> 3. Track your return rate and top return reason, and fix the biggest one first.
>
> **Words to know:**
>
> - **3PL:** Third-party logistics: a warehouse company that stores your stock and packs and ships your orders.
> - **Return rate:** The share of orders that customers send back.
> - **Shipping protection:** An optional fee at checkout that covers lost, stolen or damaged parcels.
> - **Lead time:** How long it takes from placing an order with your supplier to having stock ready to sell.
>
> All words are explained in the [glossary](../glossary.md).

## Lesson 23.1: Do you need a 3PL yet?

Packing orders yourself is cheap at first and expensive later, because your hours stop going into growth. A 3PL (a warehouse company that stores and ships for you) makes sense when packing takes more of your week than selling does.

**Do this:**

1. For one normal week, write down how many orders you sent and how many hours packing, labelling and dropping off took.
2. Work out your cost per order today: packaging, labels, postage and your time at a fair hourly rate.
3. Ask at least three warehouses for a quote using the same sample month: your orders per month, units per order, number of products, carton sizes and how many pallets or shelves of stock you hold.
4. Compare the full monthly bill, not just the pick-and-pack line. Ask about receiving fees, storage (per pallet, shelf or cubic metre), packaging, minimum monthly charges, set-up fees, returns handling and peak-season surcharges.
5. Ask for a sample invoice from a client of a similar size. It shows the fees that the price sheet leaves out.
6. Ask three service questions: the daily order cut-off time, the error rate they measure, and what they do when they make a mistake.
7. Check the store connection: orders should flow in from Shopify automatically and tracking should flow back.

**Australia:** most warehouses sit in Sydney, Melbourne or Brisbane. Ask for delivery times to Perth and regional areas, because those are where complaints start. If most customers are on the east coast, one warehouse is usually enough.

**US:** one warehouse in the middle of the country reaches most addresses in 2 to 5 days by ground. Two warehouses (one per coast) cut delivery time but split your stock. Stock held in a state can mean you owe sales tax there, so read [Lesson 26.3](26-tax-gst-sales-tax-duties.md#lesson-263-us-sales-tax-nexus-registering-and-filing) first.

**Self-check:** do you know your true cost per order today, including your time? Do your three quotes compare the same month of orders?

## Lesson 23.2: Moving to a 3PL without losing orders

Most moves go wrong in the handover week: stock counts do not match, barcodes are missing or orders get stuck between systems.

**Do this:**

1. Give every product and variant a unique SKU and a barcode before anything ships to the warehouse. See [Module 22](22-shopify-store-setup.md).
2. Send a delivery notice with each shipment: what is in each carton, by SKU and quantity. Most warehouses charge more for cartons that arrive without one.
3. Ask the warehouse to count everything on arrival and send you the count. Fix gaps before you switch.
4. Pick a quiet day to switch, never the week before a sale.
5. Place three test orders: one item, several items, and an address in a remote area. Check tracking reaches the customer.
6. For the first month, compare the warehouse stock report with Shopify every week. Investigate any gap bigger than a few units.
7. Write down who to call when something goes wrong, and how fast they promise to answer.

**Australia and US:** keep a small amount of stock at home for the first month so you can send replacements quickly if something goes wrong.

**Self-check:** did the warehouse count match what you sent? Did all three test orders arrive with tracking?

## Lesson 23.3: Shipping rates, carriers and delays

Shipping is often the second biggest cost after the product. Know it before you set prices and free shipping rules.

**Do this:**

1. Weigh and measure your three most common parcels. Carriers often charge by size as well as weight (called cubic or dimensional weight), so smaller boxes can save money.
2. Get rates from at least two carriers for your top delivery zones.
3. Work out your average shipping cost per order from last month's invoices. Put it in Helix under shipping so profit is right.
4. Choose a rule: free shipping over an amount, a flat rate, or real-time rates. Set any free shipping amount a little above your average order. See [Lesson 9.11](09-website-and-cro.md#lesson-911-shipping-psychology).
5. Show delivery times on product pages and at checkout. Clear expectations prevent most "where is my order" emails.
6. Make a disruption plan: a second carrier you can switch to, an email you can send in minutes, and a banner for your store. Postal services do pause routes, change prices or slow down at peak season.
7. Review carrier prices every time they announce a rise, and at least once a year.

**Australia:** compare the national post with private couriers, especially for parcels over 1 kg and for Western Australia and regional areas. Price rises usually come once or twice a year, so update your shipping rates when they do.

**US:** compare USPS, UPS and FedEx for your zones. Ground services are often cheaper for heavier parcels; USPS is often cheaper for small, light ones.

**Self-check:** do you know your average shipping cost per order, and is it in your Helix numbers? Do you have a second carrier ready?

## Lesson 23.4: Shipping protection and lost or damaged parcels

Some parcels will go missing or arrive broken. Decide in advance who pays, how fast you fix it and whether you offer optional protection at checkout.

**Do this:**

1. Track lost and damaged parcels for a month: how many, what they cost you, and which carrier.
2. Write a simple rule: for example, you resend or refund once a carrier marks a parcel lost or a customer sends a photo of damage.
3. Claim from the carrier when you can. Keep proof of value and photos of packaging.
4. If you offer shipping protection (an optional fee that covers loss or damage), make it a clear opt-in: unticked by default, with the price shown. Shopify does not allow apps to pre-select paid add-ons at checkout. See [Shopify app store requirements](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/shopify-app-store/app-store-requirements).
5. Count protection fees as income and your resends as costs, so you can see if it pays for itself.
6. Improve packaging for anything that breaks more than once.

**Australia:** pre-selected extras can count as part of the headline price under Australian price display rules, and hiding fees until checkout is a known problem area. See [ACCC: price displays](https://www.accc.gov.au/business/pricing/price-displays). New Zealand also takes a firm view on pre-ticked extras, so keep them opt-in everywhere.

**US:** the [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing) treats hidden or pre-checked charges as a risk area. Keep any add-on clear and optional.

**Self-check:** do you know what lost and damaged parcels cost you last month? Is any protection fee opt-in and clearly priced?

## Lesson 23.5: Returns and exchanges that cost less

Every return costs you twice: the refund and the handling. Your goal is fewer returns and more exchanges, while always meeting the law.

**Do this:**

1. Work out your return rate: returned orders divided by orders, for the last 3 months. Track it by product.
2. Add a reason to every return (too small, too big, not as pictured, faulty, changed mind). Fix the top reason first.
3. Lower returns at the source: a clear size guide, real photos, measurements, and reviews that mention fit.
4. Offer an exchange or store credit first, with a refund still available where the law requires it.
5. Make returns easy to start (a form or portal) but check the portal's monthly price against how many returns you process.
6. Inspect returned items quickly and put sellable stock back on sale.
7. Put refunds into Helix so profit is right.

**Australia:** the Australian Consumer Law gives customers rights to a repair, replacement or refund when a product has a problem, no matter what your policy says. Change-of-mind returns are your choice. Never write "no refunds". See [ACCC: consumer guarantees](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/buying-products-and-services/consumer-rights-and-guarantees).

**US:** there is no single federal rule for change-of-mind returns, but you must honour the policy you publish and some states have their own rules. Many US shoppers expect a local returns address, so ask your warehouse to receive returns. See the [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance).

**Self-check:** do you know your return rate and your top return reason? Does your policy meet the law in each country you sell to?

## Lesson 23.6: When a spreadsheet stops working for stock

A spreadsheet works for a handful of products. It breaks when you have many variants, more than one location, or purchase orders in transit.

**Do this:**

1. Start with the Helix stock page: [Suppliers and stock](/dashboard/stock) shows reorder points, stock-out dates and order-by dates. See [Lesson 14.9](14-inventory-cash-planning.md).
2. Move to inventory software when two or more of these are true: more than about 100 variants, more than one warehouse, frequent stock mistakes, or you spend hours each week on stock.
3. Before you buy, list what you need: purchase orders, landed cost, multiple locations, bundles, and a link to your accounting software.
4. Ask for a trial with your own data. Check it handles bundles and returns correctly.
5. Count stock fully at least once a year (see [Lesson 27.3](27-accounting-bookkeeping-tax-time.md#lesson-273-cost-of-goods-and-stock-in-your-books)) and spot-check your best sellers each month.

**Australia and US:** whichever tool you pick, keep one source of truth for stock. Two systems that disagree are worse than one simple sheet.

**Self-check:** do you know which of the four signs apply to you? Is there one place where your stock numbers live?
